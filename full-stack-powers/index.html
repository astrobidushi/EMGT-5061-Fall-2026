The Full-Stack Powers

Global Space Ecosystems I: the United States, Europe, China, Russia, India and Japan. Each can build, launch and operate almost everything it needs in space. But how full is "full stack" when one rocket fails? In class you stress-test the six powers, then start mapping New Mexico's own space assets. For homework you finish the map.

How we compare the powers

Today's lecture looks at every country through the same seven questions. Use them in both exercises.

1. Governance

Who runs the space program, and where does it sit in government?

2. Money

How big is the budget, and who pays: civil, military or commercial?

3. Access to space

Can it launch its own satellites and people? On how many rockets, from how many pads?

4. Flagship programs

What does it fly that others notice: stations, Moon missions, navigation?

5. Commercial policy tools

How does it grow companies: contracts, licensing, funds, technology transfer?

6. International alignment

Who are its partners? Artemis Accords, ILRS, both or neither?

7. Workforce

Where do its engineers come from, and can it keep them?

Small Group Exercise 1

Stress test: how full is full stack?

Each shock card below puts one of the six full-stack powers in a real crisis: its main way to reach orbit is suddenly gone. What happens next? All six events really happened.

How this works

You're running a country's space program when disaster strikes. Your group is assigned two cards.

Dependence costs more than money. When a country relies on someone else to reach orbit, it can also pay in:

  • Leverage: the provider can use your need as a bargaining chip.
  • Schedule: you fly when they can fit you in, not when you want to.
  • Skills: your own workforce and industry shrink while you wait.
  • Prestige and autonomy: you can't claim independence in space.
  • Security: your satellites or astronauts sit inside someone else's system.

We put it all together at the end. Each group reports back on its two cards, and then the whole class ranks all six powers from most to least resilient.

Group A

Group A · Card A1

China

July 2, 2017

The second Long March 5, China's most powerful rocket, fails minutes after liftoff because of a fault in a first-stage engine. China's lunar sample return mission, its space station modules and its first Mars mission all need this rocket.

Source, to open after you answer in your student deck: AP via Phys.org: China rocket failure likely to set back next space missions

Group A · Card A2

Europe

July 2023

The last Ariane 5 flies. Its replacement, Ariane 6, is years late. The smaller Vega-C has been grounded since a failure in December 2022. Russia pulled its Soyuz rockets out of Europe's spaceport in 2022. ESA has satellites ready to go.

Source, to open after you answer in your student deck: ESA: Ariane 5 bows out in style

Group B

Group B · Card B1

India

May 2025 and January 2026

PSLV, India's workhorse rocket with a long record of successes, fails twice in eight months, both times in its third stage. The second flight was a commercial mission carrying an Earth observation satellite and 15 smaller satellites, some for foreign customers.

Source, to open after you answer in your student deck: NASASpaceflight: India's PSLV and its second third-stage failure

Group B · Card B2

Japan

December 22, 2025

Japan's flagship H3 rocket fails: separating the nose cone damages the satellite's mount, and the Michibiki 5 navigation satellite is lost. Japan retired its older H-IIA rocket in June 2025.

Source, to open after you answer in your student deck: JAXA: Launch failure of the 8th H3 launch vehicle

Group C

Group C · Card C1

Russia

November 27, 2025

The Soyuz MS-28 crew reaches orbit safely, but the launch collapses the mobile service platform at Baikonur's Site 31 into the flame trench below the pad. Site 31 is Russia's only pad for sending crews and cargo to the ISS.

Source, to open after you answer in your student deck: RussianSpaceWeb: Baikonur Site 31 accident

Group C · Card C2

United States

July 2011

The Space Shuttle flies its last mission. NASA has no other vehicle that can launch its astronauts, and the International Space Station still needs crews. The replacement spacecraft are years away.

Source, to open after you answer in your student deck: NASA: STS-135, the space shuttle's grand finale

TRL, MRL and the value chain

For worked TRL and MRL examples, see Ready for the Moon?

TRL definitions (1–9)
TRLTechnology Readiness (NASA)
MRL definitions (0–9)
MRLMarket Readiness (Mealing, Starbridge Venture Capital)

TRL asks "does it work?" MRL asks "has the market been tested: who are the customers, do they want it, and will they pay?" A product can be TRL 9 and still be MRL 3 if no one has tested who will buy it.
