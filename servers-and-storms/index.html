What space weather does to a data center

Storms add drag

A geomagnetic storm heats the upper atmosphere and makes it swell. Satellites in low orbit slow down and sink faster. In February 2022, a minor storm brought down 38 new Starlink satellites.

Single particles cause errors

One cosmic ray or solar proton crossing a chip can flip a bit (a wrong 1 or 0) or trigger a latchup (a short that stays on until the power is cut). Shielding doesn't stop the most energetic particles.

Dose builds up

Years of radiation slowly damage a chip's insulating layers until its transistors stop switching cleanly. Shielding helps here. How fast the dose builds depends on the site.

The Van Allen radiation belts

Earth's magnetic field traps charged particles in two doughnut-shaped belts. A trapped particle spirals around a magnetic field line, bounces between the north and south "mirror points" where the field gets stronger (about once a second), and slowly drifts around the planet. Together these motions fill out the belts.

  • Inner belt: energetic protons, tens to hundreds of MeV, from roughly 1,000 km out to about 6,000 km or a little beyond, above the equator. They are most intense around 3,000 km. They pass through shielding and cause both dose damage and bit flips.
  • Outer belt: mostly energetic electrons, from roughly 13,000 km outward, swelling and shrinking with solar storms. They build up electric charge on and inside spacecraft.
  • Latitude matters. The belts follow the field lines, so they sit highest over the equator and dip lower toward the poles (the "horns"). Over the polar caps the field lines open into space, so nothing is trapped there, but particles from solar storms can stream straight down.
  • The South Atlantic Anomaly. Earth's magnetic field is tilted and off-center. Over South America and the South Atlantic it is weaker, and the inner belt dips to about 200 km. Satellites in low orbit cross it several times a day.

Sources differ on exactly where each belt begins and ends; the figures here are approximate.

How data moves through a space data center

1. Up

From the customer's ground station by radio or laser, or through a relay satellite. Data from other satellites can arrive by laser links between satellites, without touching the ground. Uplinks are usually slower than downlinks.

→

2. In space

Data is written to solid-state drives, processed by GPUs or TPUs, and the results are stored until the next downlink. Everything runs on solar power and sheds heat through radiators. Error-correcting memory and regular saves of progress handle bit flips.

→

3. Down

By laser or radio during passes over ground stations, continuously from GEO, or through a relay from the Moon. Clouds block laser links, so operators need several ground stations in different weather zones.

Rule of thumb: the link to the ground is the narrowest part of the pipe. Computing in space makes the most sense when the data starts in space, or when the results are much smaller than the input.

Homework 2

Check the pitch: power and heat

Engage Compute is raising money for a 1 MW module in LEO. Its pitch deck says, "Power is free and cooling in space is free." Check both claims with two rules of thumb.

Two rules of thumb

Solar power. Sunlight in Earth orbit delivers 1,361 W per square metre. Good space solar cells turn about 30% of that into electricity, so each m² of array gives about 400 W.

Getting rid of heat. Nearly all the electricity a computer uses ends up as heat. In a vacuum there is no air or water to carry it away, so heat can only leave as infrared radiation from a radiator panel. A two-sided panel at about room temperature (27 °C) radiates about 800 W per m².

Area needed = power ÷ watts per m²

These ignore sunlight and Earth's heat landing on the radiator, which make the real radiator larger.
