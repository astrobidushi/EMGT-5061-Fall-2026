The two ladders: is it ready, and will anyone buy it?

Meet Polar Well

Polar Well is a startup that wants to mine water ice at the Moon’s south pole and sell it as rocket propellant. It says its solar thermal reactor can extract 160 tons of water ice a year, and it is asking investors for $80M to build the flight model. It must also choose what to sell: water on the lunar surface, or water delivered to low Earth orbit. You will meet Polar Well again in Part 2, and Nightfall Power after it.

Polar Well is fictional. Its claims and figures are simplified for teaching.

Meet Nightfall Power

Nightfall Power is a startup building a small fission reactor to power lunar bases and mining sites through the 14-day lunar night, when solar panels produce nothing. Its would-be customers are NASA, lunar mining startups and lander companies. The founder tells your firm the company is “almost ready.”

Nightfall Power is fictional. Its milestones are simplified for teaching.

      Market terms used above

      Value proposition: a short statement of who the customer is, what problem or need the product solves for them, and why it does so better than the alternatives they have today, by enough that they would pay for it.

      Business Model Canvas: a one-page chart that lays out how a company will make money, in nine boxes. The customer side covers customer segments, the value proposition, channels, customer relationships and revenue streams. The other side covers key activities, key resources, key partners and the cost structure. Each box starts as a guess, and the team tests the guesses with real customers. See the canvas (Strategyzer).

      Minimum viable product (MVP): the simplest version of a product that lets the team learn whether customers really want it, with the least time and money.

      Low-fidelity market test (MRL 4–5): showing customers a simple stand-in for the product, such as a spec sheet, a draft contract or a price list. It is cheap and fast, but it measures what customers say, not what they do. For Polar Well: prospective customers review a spec sheet, a draft purchase contract and a price list per kilogram, and the team counts letters of intent. For Nightfall: prospective customers review the reactor spec sheet, a draft power-purchase contract and a price per kilowatt-hour, and the team counts letters of intent.

      High-fidelity market test (MRL 6–7): putting customers in contact with something that works much like the real product, usually a full prototype, often with money changing hands. It costs far more, but it shows how customers actually behave. For Polar Well: a customer pays for water made by the full-scale ground prototype and runs it through its own propellant system. For Nightfall: a customer pays to run its own equipment on power from the full-scale ground prototype. Either test can’t start until the technology reaches TRL 6.

      Part 2

      Exercises: Polar Well and Nightfall Power

      Polar Well: lunar infrastructure and resource extraction

      From ice to propellant, in five steps

      • Prospect: drills and spectrometers find where the ice is, and how much.
      • Excavate: rovers dig icy soil in craters that never see sunlight.
      • Extract: heat the soil and capture the water vapor.
      • Process: purify the water, split it into hydrogen and oxygen, liquefy and store them.
      • Deliver: load the propellant into a customer’s lander.

      Where things stand (Oct 2026)

      • No government or company has ever harvested lunar ice.
      • NASA’s VIPER rover, built to map the ice, is due to land in late 2027.
      • In April 2026 Blue Origin made oxygen from simulated lunar soil, a route that needs no ice.
      • Interlune has signed buyers for lunar helium-3; no one has signed a contract to buy lunar propellant.
      Where Polar Well fits: Polar Well’s extractor covers the Extract step. Before any of its water earns money, the steps around it have to work too, and a customer has to sign up for the Deliver step.

      Exercise 2

      Polar Well’s $80M pitch

      First, some background on the market for lunar resources:

      • In 2016, United Launch Alliance (ULA) said it would pay $500/kg for propellant on the lunar surface, $1,000/kg at the Earth–Moon L1 point and $3,000/kg in low Earth orbit.
      • Starpath Robotics is designing a plant to make liquid oxygen from lunar ice.
      • Interlune has signed agreements, worth close to $500M in total, to sell lunar helium-3 to Bluefors, Maybell Quantum and the U.S. Department of Energy.

      Then Polar Well pitches your firm:

      “We’ve developed a revolutionary solar thermal reactor that can extract 160 tons of water ice per year on the Moon, but we need $80M in venture funding to build the flight model.”

      Polar Well can sell its water in one of two ways:

      Option A: Surface onlyOption B: LEO delivery
      Output160 metric tons a year, sold on the lunar surface50 metric tons a year, delivered to low Earth orbit
      Price$500/kg$3,000/kg
      Potential revenue$80M a year$150M a year

      Both options mine the same ice. In this simplified case, only 50 tons reach low Earth orbit because some of the water is used up as propellant to ship it there.

      Nightfall Power: space nuclear power

      Four technologies, one word

      • Radioisotope power: heat from decaying plutonium-238 (or americium-241, strontium-90) makes a few hundred watts. Flown for decades, including on Curiosity and Perseverance.
      • Fission surface power: a small reactor on the Moon or Mars, 20–100+ kW. This is Nightfall’s business.
      • Nuclear electric propulsion: a reactor powers electric thrusters.
      • Nuclear thermal propulsion: a reactor heats hydrogen into a rocket exhaust.

      Where things stand (Oct 2026)

      • The U.S. has flown one reactor in space: SNAP-10A, in 1965.
      • NASA plans SR-1 Freedom, a 20 kW reactor spacecraft, for Mars in late 2028.
      • U.S. policy targets a lunar surface reactor ready to launch by 2030 (NASA’s Lunar Reactor-1) and a military in-space reactor by 2031.
      • DARPA and NASA canceled the DRACO nuclear thermal rocket in 2025 after cheaper launch weakened its case.
      • The buyers today are government agencies, for example the Space Force’s $161M award to Antares in September 2026.
      Where Nightfall fits: NASA’s Lunar Reactor-1 asks for at least 20 kWe delivered up to 1 km from the reactor, with landing targeted for 2030. U.S. policy also requires firm fixed-price contracts with milestone payments, so a company like Nightfall would carry its own cost overruns.

      Start here: Aerospace America, “Spotlight on space nuclear” · POWER, the 2026 White House space nuclear initiative · Lockheed Martin, why reactors for the lunar night

      Exercise 3

      Two offers on the table

      Nightfall Power has two offers. NASA proposes a firm fixed-price contract, with payments at milestones, for a reactor delivering at least 20 kWe on the Moon by 2030. Separately, Polar Well offers to sign a letter of intent to buy Nightfall’s power for its ice-mining site once that site is operating.

      Both offers are fictional. The 20 kWe, 2030 target and the fixed-price, milestone-payment terms come from NASA’s Lunar Reactor-1 effort and current U.S. policy.
