How a debris warning plays out

  1. 1

    A warning arrives

    Three days before a close pass, tracking shows a piece of debris heading near the platform. Its exact position isn't known yet: it could be anywhere along a stretch of its path that can be kilometres long. The collision odds are the chance that the platform will be inside that stretch when the debris passes.

  2. 2

    Each day, the picture gets clearer

    Every time a radar sees the debris again, its predicted position gets sharper and the stretch shrinks. Usually it shrinks to a spot away from the platform, and the warning clears. Sometimes it shrinks around the platform's path, the odds go up, and Stillwater has to burn. That's why waiting a day can be worth it, and why only 18 of about 60 warnings a year end in a burn.

  3. 3

    Better tracking clears it sooner

    Stillwater uses free public data and does not subscribe to Keelwatch. Keelwatch's radars see the debris more often and more precisely, so the picture sharpens faster and the odds come out clearer: either very low or clearly high. In class you decide on three warnings with free data first, then again with Keelwatch's data.

The conjunction log

You are Stillwater's operations manager. Three debris warnings came in over a recent 12-day stretch. For each one, decide whether to start the next batch now or hold it for a day, and log your decision and reason.

How to decide

Each warning arrives 3 days before the closest approach, just as the next batch is due to start. You can start the batch now, or hold it a day while the debris's position becomes clearer. The tables below tell you how often a warning ends in a burn, using free public data or Keelwatch's data.

Risk cost

Risk cost = probability of the loss × size of the loss. You compare that number with the cost of the alternative, usually the cost of preventing or avoiding the loss. If prevention costs less than the risk cost, you prevent. If it costs more, you accept the risk. NASA's risk matrices rest on the same idea, rating each risk by likelihood and consequence.

With no warning, there's nothing to weigh. The calculation only starts when a specific risk shows up.

Start the batch now

If the warning ends in a burn, the batch is ruined. That costs $1.0M.

Hold one day

Wait a day for clearer tracking, then start. In this exercise, one day is enough for the warning to settle: by then you know whether Stillwater must burn, and any burn happens before the batch begins. So a held batch is never ruined. You lose one day of a 4-day cycle: $0.7M ÷ 4 = $0.175M.

The dodge line never changes. Stillwater always burns when the collision odds are above 1 in 10,000, because its insurance is void with any looser line. Your decision is about something else: whether a batch is growing when a burn might happen.

The rule: compare the risk cost of starting the batch now with the cost of holding it a day

  1. Cost of holding one day. A batch cycle is 4 days, and a good batch earns $0.7M, so each day of the cycle is worth $0.7M ÷ 4 = $0.175M. Holding loses one day, so it always costs $0.175M. In return, the batch is safe.
  2. Risk cost of starting the batch now. If the warning ends in a burn, the batch is ruined, which costs $1.0M. If the warning clears, the batch finishes normally and costs nothing extra. To compare this with holding, weigh the $1.0M by how likely it is: risk cost = chance of a burn × $1.0M. A 30% chance of losing $1.0M, for example, has a risk cost of $0.30M.
  3. Compare the two. Hold if the risk cost of starting the batch now is more than the cost of holding it for one day; otherwise start. The tables above give the risk cost for each kind of warning.

Example: with a 25% chance of a burn, the risk cost of starting the batch now is 25% × $1.0M = $0.25M, more than the $0.175M cost of holding, so hold. With a 5% chance, the risk cost is $0.05M, less than $0.175M, so start.

Homework 1

Fix the orbit or fix the process?

Stillwater has asked your firm to fund one fix for its debris problem. Compare the options using profit per year.

What "profit per year" means

It's what Stillwater makes from its batches in a normal year: what Halden pays for good batches, minus consumables, minus any yearly fee the option adds. It leaves out the platform's own cost, staff and overhead, which are the same under every option.

Profit per year = (good batches × $0.7M) − (ruined batches × $0.3M) − any yearly fee

Each burn ruins one batch, so ruined batches = burns a year, and the rest of the 90 slots are good batches. One-time costs, such as moving the platform, are listed in their own column, not taken out of profit per year.

Example, today's baseline: 72 good × $0.7M − 18 ruined × $0.3M = $45.0M.
