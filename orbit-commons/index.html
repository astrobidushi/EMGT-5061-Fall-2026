Questions

Part 2: An orbital-use fee

Some economists propose charging operators a fee for every satellite they keep in orbit. One study (Rao, Burgess & Kaffine, PNAS, 2020) proposed a fee that rises annually to about $235,000 per satellite per year by 2040. On that path, the fee in 2026 works out to about $37,500 per satellite. Suppose that fee applies to your company's fleet starting in 2026.

  1. What would the fee cost each option per year in 2026? Keep in mind that it rises to $235,000 per satellite per year by 2040.
  2. Which option does the fee favor, and why?
  3. Is that the outcome a regulator worried about debris would want? Suggest one way to design the fee so it rewards responsible disposal.

About the model

This is a teaching model, not a forecast. It follows the structure of published source-sink debris models such as MIT's MOCAT: launches add satellites, drag and disposal remove objects, and collisions turn intact objects into fragments. It collapses low Earth orbit into two orbits, lower (about 400–700 km, modeled at 550 km) and higher (about 700–1,000 km, modeled at 900 km), and steps forward in fractions of a year. Starting counts come from ESA. The rates inside the model are tuned so it behaves the way published studies describe, and the table marks which values are which.

In every run, today's 17,000 satellites stay in orbit: operators replace them as they wear out, so the number of active satellites never drops below today's level. The launch setting and the scenarios only add new satellites on top of that fleet. Two other simplifications matter most. Each orbit is treated as evenly mixed, so any two objects in the same orbit can collide. And fragments in lower orbit fall back within a couple of years, so a runaway cascade can only develop in higher orbit. Real studies find the same pattern: altitudes near 900–1,000 km are where debris can feed on itself.

Sources

Every source used to build this page, and what each one supplied.

  • Jonathan McDowell, active satellite statistics, as of 30 September 2026. The count of about 17,000 operating satellites and their recent growth of about 3,000 a year.
  • ESA, Space Environment Statistics (DISCOS), as of 31 July 2026. Starting counts for derelicts, rocket bodies, and fragments in LEO.
  • ESA, Space Environment Report 2026. The finding that debris growth is accelerating.
  • ESA, Space Environment Report 2025. The default 90% disposal rate and the finding that debris keeps growing even with no new launches.
  • Liou, J.-C. & Johnson, N. L., "Risks in Space from Orbiting Debris," Science 311 (2006), and the NASA paper behind it. The "launches stop" test, the 5.2% yearly collision rate used to calibrate the model, and the finding that the 900–1,000 km altitude range is the most unstable.
  • MIT ARCLab, open-source MOCAT space debris model (2024) and its code on GitHub. The model's structure: launches in, drag and disposal out, collisions turning intact objects into fragments.
  • Rao, A., Burgess, M. G. & Kaffine, D., "Orbital-use fees could more than quadruple the value of the space industry," PNAS 117 (2020). The fee path: rising 14% a year to $235,000 per satellite-year in 2040.
  • Federal Communications Commission, FCC 22-74, Second Report and Order (2022). The 5-year deorbit rule, compared with the older 25-year guideline.
  • 2009 Iridium–Cosmos collision (Wikipedia, citing U.S. tracking data). About 1,500 tracked fragments still in orbit seven years later, used to check fragments per collision.
