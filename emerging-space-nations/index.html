Four playbooks

Last week's full-stack powers can build, launch and operate almost everything themselves. Today's countries can't, so each chooses where to compete. We look at all of them through the same seven framework questions, listed below the playbooks.

Regulators

Luxembourg, New Zealand

Small countries that win with laws. Clear rules and fast licensing bring companies to them from abroad.

Hubs

Singapore, United Kingdom

Places where space companies and money gather: finance, insurance, talent and partnerships with bigger programs.

Nation-builders

UAE, Australia

Countries using space to build a new industry, a workforce and national pride, often by buying help from abroad at first.

Climbers

South Korea, Senegal

Countries climbing step by step toward full stack. South Korea is far up the ladder: its own rockets, its own satellites, and now the Moon. Senegal is on the first rung: a first satellite built with a partner, the next one on its own.

Each country is listed under the playbook it leans on most today, but most do more than one thing:

  • Luxembourg (regulator) is also a hub: it's home to SES, one of the world's largest satellite operators, and the government is its biggest shareholder.
  • New Zealand (regulator) also launches: Rocket Lab flies from its soil.
  • Singapore (hub) also builds satellites: ST Engineering's TeLEOS-1 (2015) and its radar satellite TeLEOS-2 (2023), both launched on Indian rockets.
  • United Kingdom (hub) also licenses launches and has a spaceport in Cornwall.
  • UAE (nation-builder) also explores deep space, and spreads the work across partners: after Hope at Mars, its MBR Explorer is set to launch to the asteroid belt in March 2028 on Japan's H3 rocket, with the University of Colorado's LASP as a technical partner, while the mission's asteroid lander and its Sirb radar satellites are built at home.
  • Australia (nation-builder) also regulates launches: it licensed its first orbital spaceport and rocket, Gilmour Space's Eris, whose first launch attempt failed in 2025.
  • South Korea (climber) already flies its own rocket, Nuri, and also still uses foreign rockets.
  • Senegal (climber) also spreads its bets across partners: its first satellite, GaindéSat-1A, was built with a French university and launched on a SpaceX rocket in 2024, and Senegal belongs to both Moon clubs (see Two Moon clubs).

The seven framework questions

  • Governance: who runs the space program, and where does it sit in government?
  • Money: how big is the budget, and who pays: civil, military or commercial?
  • Access to space: can it launch its own satellites and people, or does it buy launches abroad?
  • Flagship programs: what does it fly that others notice?
  • Commercial policy tools: how does it grow companies: contracts, licensing, funds, grants?
  • International alignment: who are its partners? Artemis Accords, ILRS, both or neither?
  • Workforce: where do its engineers come from, and can it keep them?

Two Moon clubs: the Artemis Accords and the ILRS

The Artemis Accords

The Artemis Accords are a set of principles, drafted by NASA and the US State Department, for peaceful and responsible exploration of the Moon, Mars, comets and asteroids. They aren't a treaty and cost nothing to sign. Signers commit to:

  • being open about what they do in space, and releasing their scientific data
  • building systems that can work with each other's, and helping astronauts in distress
  • protecting historic sites, planning for the safe disposal of debris, and setting up "safety zones" so missions don't interfere with one another
  • following shared rules for extracting and using resources found in space

What a country gets: a voice in writing the rules for the Moon, and a door into NASA's Artemis program. In March 2026 NASA announced a Moon Base at the lunar south pole, to be built with companies and partner countries. Signing opens that door; the actual role (contracts, instruments on missions, astronaut seats, hardware for the base) comes through a separate deal with NASA.

Australia

A founding signer, October 2020. Its agency is building a small rover, Roo-ver, to go to the Moon with NASA. Roo-ver will scoop up lunar soil so NASA can try to extract oxygen from it, and is set to fly on a US commercial lander by 2030.

UAE

A founding signer, October 2020. Its planned airlock for NASA's Gateway was cancelled in March 2026. NASA has replaced Gateway with a Moon Base.

Senegal

Signed in July 2025, the 56th country to join and the fourth in Africa. It had already joined the ILRS ten months earlier (see below).

The International Lunar Research Station (ILRS)

The ILRS is a plan led by China and Russia, which signed an agreement in March 2021, to build a permanent research base near the Moon's south pole. Construction is planned for 2026 to 2035, starting with China's Chang'e-7 and Chang'e-8 missions. Unlike the Artemis Accords, its rules for members haven't been made public.

Who has joined: 13 countries as of July 2026. They are China, Russia, Azerbaijan, Belarus, Egypt, Kazakhstan, Nicaragua, Pakistan, Senegal, Serbia, South Africa, Thailand and Venezuela. China's space agency gives a bigger number, "17 countries and international organizations," because it also counts groups such as the Asia-Pacific Space Cooperation Organization.

What members expect to get: a role in a Moon base they could never build alone, such as room for an instrument or a small rover on a Chinese lunar mission, or running a ground station (Venezuela's role), plus Chinese training and technology. China is aiming for 50 countries, 500 research institutions and 5,000 scientists.

Artemis AccordsILRS
Pros
  • Free to sign, with published principles
  • The bigger club: 78 countries as of October 2026
  • A door into NASA's Artemis program and its planned Moon Base: contracts, instruments on missions, even astronaut seats
  • Joining means joining the Moon base project itself, with room on Chinese lunar missions
  • Chinese training and technology, often for countries with little access to US programs
  • China has already landed three times on the Moon and brought back samples
Cons
  • Signing alone brings no money or seats: each benefit is a separate deal with NASA
  • Lines a country up with the United States
  • NASA can change course: when it cancelled Gateway in 2026, partners' hardware, like the UAE's airlock, lost its home
  • Critics say its rules on using Moon resources stretch the Outer Space Treaty's ban on claiming territory
  • Its rules for members aren't public
  • The smaller club: 13 countries
  • US export rules can block a country from putting US-linked parts on Chinese missions
  • Schedule risk: Chang'e-7 was postponed in August 2026 with no new date

You can join both. Thailand (2024), Senegal (2025) and Serbia (2026) belong to both clubs, and the US says ILRS members are welcome to sign the Accords.

Partner diversification. For developing space nations like these three, joining both clubs is a strategy, not an accident. Signing the Artemis Accords (78 signers) and joining the ILRS (13 member countries) lets an emerging program reach scientific data, payload opportunities and ground station networks in both of the major space coalitions, instead of depending on one.

Australia

Senegal

  • ILRS: joined September 5, 2024, through an agreement between its space agency, ASES, and China's space agency. The deal includes training Senegalese scientists and engineers for lunar missions.
  • Artemis Accords: signed July 24, 2025, at NASA headquarters.

Surviving the lunar night: RHUs and RTGs

A night at the Moon's south pole lasts about two weeks and is bitterly cold. NASA's Moon Base plan, announced at its "Ignition" event in March 2026, tests nuclear heat sources in its first phase, alongside solar power and, later, a fission reactor. Two kinds of nuclear device are often confused:

Radioisotope heater unit (RHU)Radioisotope thermoelectric generator (RTG)
MakesHeat only, about 1 watt eachElectricity, tens to hundreds of watts
SizeAbout 40 grams, the size of a C-cell batteryAbout 45 kilograms for a Mars rover's RTG
JobKeeps instruments and batteries from freezingPowers a spacecraft or rover
ExamplesTwo 15-watt heaters warmed Apollo 11's seismometer in 1969; Cassini carried 82 RHUsCassini carried three; Curiosity and Perseverance on Mars run on one each

Both use the heat of decaying plutonium-238, and neither is new. What's new is the fuel: Zeno Power's 5-watt americium-241 RHU is set to fly on a Firefly lander no earlier than 2028. Americium is easier to get than scarce plutonium-238. Why it matters for emerging nations: these heat sources take nuclear fuel and government approvals, so a country can't simply buy one. A partner that can supply them is what lets a payload survive the lunar night.
